Part A

Principles of Justice

Rule of LawPrinciple 1
Everyone — including citizens, police, politicians and government officials — is subject to the law. Laws should be applied fairly and consistently.
Apply it to Banksia Creek: Alex, Claire and Councillor Reed must all be treated according to the same legal process, regardless of age, job or public position.
Ask: Has anyone been treated as guilty before the evidence was properly tested?
Presumption of InnocencePrinciple 2
A person accused of a criminal offence is treated as innocent unless and until guilt is established according to law.
Apply it: Being present at the community centre, being mentioned by a witness or being criticised in the media does not prove a person committed an offence.
Ask: Which pieces of evidence create suspicion, and which actually establish a fact?
Burden of ProofPrinciple 3
In a criminal case, the prosecution has the responsibility of proving the charge. The accused does not have to prove that they are innocent.
Criminal standard: the prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt.
Ask: If important questions remain unanswered, could guilt be established beyond reasonable doubt?
Fair Trial & Legal RepresentationPrinciple 4
A fair legal process requires an independent court, proper procedures, an opportunity for evidence to be tested, and the ability of an accused person to obtain legal advice or representation.
Apply it: A newspaper headline, social-media rumour or community opinion cannot replace the court process.
Ask: Why is an independent court more reliable than deciding guilt through public opinion?
Part B

Possible Victorian Offences

TheftCrimes Act 1958 (Vic)
For this investigation, focus on four key ideas. Theft involves taking or dealing with property belonging to another dishonestly and with an intention to permanently deprive the owner of it.
Ask four questions:
  • Was there property belonging to another person or organisation?
  • Did someone take or deal with that property?
  • Was the conduct dishonest?
  • Did the person intend that the owner would permanently lose the property?
Banksia Creek: The $18,750 is property. But if a person genuinely believed they were temporarily moving it to protect the account, the issue of dishonesty and intention becomes very important.
Your task: Which elements could the current evidence support? Which elements are still uncertain?
Obtaining Property by DeceptionCrimes Act 1958 (Vic)
Victorian law also deals with situations where a person uses deception and dishonesty to obtain property belonging to another.
Think about:
  • Was somebody deliberately or recklessly deceptive?
  • Did that deception cause property to come under somebody else's control?
  • Was the conduct dishonest?
  • Was there an intention to permanently deprive the owner of the property?
Banksia Creek: Exhibit E may suggest that the person who created the fake security email is more legally significant than somebody who was merely present at the community centre.
Important: Do you have enough evidence to identify the sender of the email? Do not invent evidence that is not in the case file.
Part C

Where Would a Case Go?

Victoria has a hierarchy of courts. The level of court depends on the type and seriousness of the matter and the way the case proceeds.

Magistrates' Court of Victoria

Magistrates' Court

The first level of the Victorian court system. It hears all summary offences, some indictable offences and conducts committal proceedings for some more serious matters.

Higher Courts

More serious criminal matters may proceed to the County Court or Supreme Court. The High Court of Australia sits at the top of the Australian court hierarchy.

Think: Why should elected politicians not be able to tell a magistrate or judge what verdict to reach?
Part D

Parliament and Courts Make Law Differently

Parliament → Statutory Law

Parliament debates and passes legislation. An Act of Parliament sets legal rules that courts apply.

Example: The Victorian Crimes Act contains offences including theft and obtaining property by deception.

Courts → Common Law / Precedent

Courts interpret legislation and decide cases. Decisions made by higher courts can create precedents that guide or bind lower courts in later cases with similar legal issues.

Why it matters: Courts sometimes need to decide what words in legislation mean when applying them to real situations.

Apply your previous learning: If the meaning of a word in a law was unclear in the Banksia Creek case, what role could a court play?
